# Module 14 — Construction of a Training Program

A training program must be flexible and dynamic. Without ongoing assessments and modifications, a training program quickly can be deemed ineffective. The quality of a coach is not so much judged by the ability to create a training program, but rather by the ability to modify and manage it to get the most out of a client. This requires weekly feedback from clients to ensure programs are relevant and on-track in relation to the goals.

Clients will come to you with differing abilities, experience levels, goals, and starting points. Perhaps your client was working solo or with other coaches but not getting the desired results and is now three months away from the goal race. Conversely, your client may be new to exercising, unable to run more than one mile and completely deconditioned. The problem with many training programs is that they make assumptions. Some of the more common client assumptions are:

- Form and biomechanics are correct
- Base level of proficiency in regard to skill and technique
- Training program starts at the beginning of a calendar year
- Training at a particular volume prior to the start of the program
- Prior running experience
- In good physical condition
- Training for a particular event

Most training programs focus on training volume. This is primarily because if a client is unable to complete distances in training that are at or near those of the event being trained for, the likelihood of successful completion on race day is slim.

This emphasis on volume versus intensity is typically greater with new and/or deconditioned clients.

Training programs range from generic to extremely involved and detailed. The type of training programs you create for your clients are largely based on your clients' goals and should also consider their strengths and weaknesses.

An important thing to remember when developing a training program for a client is that your client is not you! Many coaches design programs around what they like and what has personally worked for them. While this approach may work in some cases, it is important not to construct training programs this way. Individuals will have varying responses to the same training stimuli. This is a critical piece that separates a qualified coach from an unqualified coach. A qualified coach will construct a training program that is grounded in science and customized for an individual client.

> **LACK OF RESEARCH**
>
> Despite the many articles in magazines and online regarding training programs for runners, there are a surprisingly low number of peer-reviewed research articles on this subject.
>
> Therefore, most articles regarding preformatted programs and training program construction are either based on the foundations of exercise science and/or based on the past experience of coaches regarding their personal experience and those they have coached.
>
> This is not necessarily a bad thing. Oftentimes the training practices that coaches implement are done because they know they work based on past experience, even if they don't know the physiological rationale behind the training methodologies. In other words, the role of sports science is often to explain why a particular training methodology works versus developing a new and groundbreaking training theory.
>
> Because of this, the majority of the content in this module is formulated via applied exercise science and evidence-based findings from our team of advisers, versus solely running programming studies.

## OBJECTIVES

Upon completion of this module, you should have an understanding of the following areas:

- Why preformatted training programs are not recommended
- Elements of running recovery
- Components of a training program
- Steps to create a training program
- Maximum recommended weekly training hours
- Challenge to the 10 percent volume increase rule
- Maximum weekly increase in volume guidelines
- Guidelines for maximum individual session volume increases
- Weekly sport-specific distribution
- When over-distance training is appropriate

The most important thing to realize when creating a training program is that there is no set formula. With so many variables, coaches must default to their knowledge of the body and their clients to create safe, progressive, and effective programs.

To illustrate this point, if you have 20 clients all training for the same race and starting training at the same time, all of the programs should be different.

Many coaches envision program creation as plug-and-play. While it is possible to use templates to create programs, this process almost all but guarantees that a program will fall short in some capacity as it is not customized.

Therefore, don't expect to go off set numbers in regard to volume increases and decreases or predesignated weekly workouts. To provide real value to your clients, you must customize the programs in all areas.

> **Preformatted Training Plans**
>
> Pick up any running magazine or training manual and you will no doubt find that it contains training programs. They give specific plans to follow to achieve one's desired results. While one of these plans might work well for one person, it probably does not work so well for others. In other words, preformatted training plans are akin to a "one size fits all" model.
>
> The primary trait that most preformatted programs have is a predetermined starting training volume. For example, a marathon-training program might assume that an athlete has been running 20 miles per week for the previous four weeks. This may or may not be the case. In regard to predetermined starting training volumes, this approach works only with someone not being professionally coached. Using the preceding example, an individual without a coach would train until reaching 20 miles/week and then would begin following the program. However, in a coach/client relationship, this buildup period to 20 miles/week is part of the training program. Therefore, the actual training program would be longer than noted in the preformatted plan.
>
> This certification will not have preformatted training plans as part of the content. Why? Preformatted programs simply do not adjust for a client's specific needs. Following are several variables that cannot be accounted for with preformatted training plans:
>
> - Recovery rate
> - Accurate training volume
> - Sport-specific strengths/weaknesses
> - Time availability
> - Physiological adaptation to training (runners adapt to the same training program at different rates and in different ways)
> - Injury
>
> It is incorrect to force potentially hundreds of different training scenarios into just a few preformatted training programs. This leads to programs that are not individualized and, therefore, nowhere near as effective as they could be. Training programs need to be individually designed for each client – period. Preformatted training programs are a great place to get ideas but should not be used "as is" for clients unless it happens to fit exactly with what a client needs.

## DELIVERY OF PROGRAM

Training programs contain a lot of information. There is often so much information presented to a client all at once that it leads to sensory overload and a lack of comprehension. Conversely, if a coach oversimplifies it, the program often leaves out critical details. What UESCA recommends is a training program that comprises three components:

1. **Full "At a Glance" Program**
2. **Weekly Training Programs**
3. **Workout Database**

By breaking down a training program into these three parts, a client is able to view the program on multiple levels and is better able to understand the individual elements of the program and how they interrelate.

> A training program works only if it is adhered to. Clients who do not stick to the plan by skipping workouts or by doing their own thing risk not meeting their goals. This is where a training log that is reported to the coach is of paramount importance, as it places accountability on the client.

As noted previously, most preformatted training programs have some sort of prerequisite in regard to existing mileage before starting a program. The exceptions are programs that assume the individual has no running experience and therefore is not currently running. A popular program such as this is the *Couch to 5K*®.

Following are the standard durations of preformatted programs (in weeks) that require some sort of mileage prerequisite:

- **Marathon:** 16–18 weeks (often relates to an 18-to-20-miles/week prerequisite)
- **Half Marathon:** 10–12 weeks (often relates to a 12-to-15-miles/week prerequisite)

While some preformatted programs are longer or shorter, it is important to know that these training durations are often what a client may have in mind. If a client is new to running and/or deconditioned, the training program duration will likely be much longer as the person does not have an existing mileage and fitness base.

## PROGRAM DESIGN TRENDS

Following are general trends concerning running program design:

1. **From the inception of training to the goal event, weekly training volumes gradually increase until the taper begins.**
   - Experienced runners will have more long runs in their program than intermediate and novice runners.
   - The longest run is typically integrated into the week before the taper.
   - Beginner runners typically perform their first long runs later in the program than intermediate and experienced runners.

2. **Deconditioned clients generally start at a low volume and intensity level and spend the majority of the training program focused on building fitness and endurance.**
   - Clients who begin a program at a moderate to high level of fitness typically have a reduced focus on fitness and aerobic development as compared with a deconditioned client.
   - Clients with a relatively high level of fitness at the inception of a training program typically integrate high-intensity-level training sooner than those with lower beginning-fitness levels.

3. **High-volume weeks are typically followed by a recovery week, especially with novice runners.**

4. **As clients progress through the training process, they should be able to handle a higher workload with faster recovery.**

5. **Generally speaking, there should not be more than two consecutive weeks of increasing volume unless the training volume is low.**

## RECOVERY

While training programs can be quite complex, the genesis of a training program is to stress the body, allow it to recover, and then stress it again. This process, repeated over and over, will allow an individual to improve health and athletic performance. The goal is to arrive at race day with the best possible fitness level, a high level of technical proficiency, and be well rested!

### EASY/HARD DAYS

Recovery between workouts is important for many reasons. Some of these reasons are:

- Decrease the chance for injury/illness
- Get the most out of future workouts
- Reduce the chance for overtraining

While a client's ability to recover must be assessed on an individual basis, it is safe to say that after any hard training day, a recovery day should follow. A recovery day may constitute an easy run or a day off. "Hard days" traditionally constitute any form of training that puts a lot of stress on the body (ex: long runs, intervals, hill repeats).

"Hard days" are subjective. For a professional marathon runner, running 17 miles at a subthreshold pace may not constitute a "hard day," while for most everyone else who does not run for a living, it most certainly is!

Because of the individualized rate of recovery, it is difficult – if not impossible – to decipher what the optimal amount of recovery (in days) is after a hard workout. There are so many variables that the best way to determine this is based on the history and progression of a client.

One day's rest after a hard day might not be sufficient for an individual to be totally recovered from a physiological standpoint. Think about it. A marathon taper is typically three weeks in duration. Why? The theory is that it takes this long for the body to recovery from all the training and to build up proper glycogen stores. It is highly probable that physiologically, the body is not completely recovered one or even several days after a hard workout. Also keep in mind that fatigue is cumulative. As a result, it is not just a singular hard workout that the body is recovering from but rather the total workload of training over a period of time.

Therefore, unless your client is tapering for a race, the goal is not to be totally recovered physiologically but rather recovered enough to be to able to perform a prescribed workout at the desired pace/distance without risking injury or illness.

#### Back-to-Back Hard Days

As noted previously, athletes will adapt to the same training stimulus in different ways. Some athletes have found that performing back-to-back hard workout sessions (high volume or high intensity) followed by successive recovery days helps to elicit a greater training adaptation response than training one hard day followed by one easy day. The exact number of recovery days that follow are based on the individual's recovery rate. However, if three or more recovery days are required, back-to-back hard days should not be implemented in a program.

This type of training should be attempted only by an athlete who recovers well on a day-to-day basis.

### SLEEP

Many physiological changes take place during sleep, such as glucose and blood pressure changes. There are also hormonal changes that according to Dattilo et al. may play a large role in a muscle's ability to recover and get stronger (721). Sleep deprivation leads to an increase in cortisol and a reduction in testosterone. As a result, protein synthesis, which is required for rebuilding damaged muscles, is greatly reduced (721).

Another study by Res et al. found that ingesting protein right before sleep aids in post-exercise recovery by stimulating muscle protein synthesis (671).

## DO WE REALLY KNOW WHAT TRAINING VOLUME IS OPTIMAL?

To some degree, there is a trickle-down effect from professional runners to age-groupers in regard to training theory and methodology. As previously mentioned, this is largely due to magazine and online articles about how pros train. Of course most age-groupers do not have the time to train at the same volume as the pros, and even if they did, most could not handle the volume without getting injured. However, the message is clear in regard to running marathons: If you want to be the best, you need to run a lot of miles … period. Most elite marathon runners average between 100 and 125 miles per week (717, 719).

*Is more always better?*

Likely not. When interviewed, many elite marathon runners often state that they are extremely conscious of the importance of recovery and therefore are careful not to overtrain. It seems as if they have identified a "sweet spot" that allows them to put in enough miles to elicit the desired training adaptations but not at the risk of becoming overtrained. So the question is, even at this self-selected "sweet spot," could they be running fewer miles and still perform at the same level … or even faster? The following diagram illustrates that the ideal training volume and intensity exists on bell curve, meaning too much or too little in regard to volume and intensity will likely result in decreased performance. The correct volume and intensity must be assessed and determined on an individual basis.

### NOT ALL MILEAGE HAS THE SAME VALUE

According to running coach Jack Daniels, increases in training volume past a certain point follow the law of diminishing returns (720). This means that increasing volume to a certain point will substantially benefit a runner. However, volume increases past this point will have a reduced benefit. In other words, you don't get the same bang for your buck. This is a slippery slope as not only are the benefits reduced, but the chance for injury also increases. This relates to the "sweet spot" and the associated diagram (figure 14.1).

### CONFLICTING INFORMATION

Historically, elite marathon runners have always trained at a high volume and therefore it has been established that this is a prerequisite to running a fast marathon. At the elite level, the phrase *If it ain't broke, don't fix it* is very applicable. For an age-grouper, changing a training program or strategy has very few repercussions. However, for professionals, there is likely too much risk in drastically altering an annual training regimen. This is likely why there is not a lot of research in the area of varied training volume among elite marathoners. As a result, high weekly mileage remains a constant among elite marathoners.

Several studies suggest that only high-intensity training leads to increases in fitness among well-conditioned aerobic athletes, and that low-intensity training does not lead to increased aerobic capacity. As the majority of time spent running by elite marathon runners is at subthreshold levels, their training methodology seems to contradict the science behind improving one's cardiovascular capacity.

Conversely, there is evidence that an increase in running volume correlates with an increase in running economy (efficiency). Running long miles also increases one's muscular endurance and mental stamina. So is the mileage run by elite marathoners a necessity or largely based on historical patterns of training, and how does this information relate to the average runner? At this point, no one really knows the answer.

### Avoid Injury

In order for an individual to finish a race, it requires being able to start it. If your client is injured and unable to start a goal race, it doesn't matter how great the previous training was. Not all injury is due to overtraining, and more specifically, there is a certain degree of randomness to training and injury. Above all else, it should be your goal to get your client to the start line on race day!

#### LONG RUNS

At the most simplistic level, more time spent running equates to more stress on the body. This is especially the case with long runs.

Individuals who introduce high-mileage days before their body is adapted put excess stress on their musculoskeletal system and therefore are at an increased risk for injury. This is especially the case for runners who have biomechanical inefficiencies and imbalances. These issues likely increase the chance for injury over those who are biomechanically sound.

A "fast" runner may be able to clip off 20 miles in training at a seven-minute-mile pace (2:20 elapsed time) while another runner may take 3:20 (10-minute-mile pace) to cover the same distance. The stress on the body for the slower runner is much greater than that of the faster runner, primarily because of duration of time spent running (one hour more).

In regard to marathon training, the average prescription for the maximum-distance long run is 20 miles. Therefore, for a slower runner, it may be advised for the long run to be 18 miles. Conversely, for faster runners and those who have a low incidence of injury, 22 miles may be the optimal long-run distance.

### Conclusions

A well-constructed training program must sufficiently stress the body but also allow for proper recovery. In regard to intensity versus volume, the ratio between the two must be configured on an individual basis. While long training runs are an important aspect to a marathon program, it is likely that an athlete in good to great aerobic condition could benefit from increased intensity as much or more from increased volume.

As a general trend, volume and intensity have an inverse relationship (figure 14.2). Therefore during a week of volume increases, it is advised not to schedule large amounts of speedwork.

## OTHER PROGRAMMING FACTORS

### TIME vs. DISTANCE (Long-Run Specific)

Many factors influence one's ability to tolerate training stress on the musculoskeletal system. You probably know a runner who can barely even look at a pair of running shoes without getting injured while another runner can seemingly run endless miles without ever getting so much as a blister! These two examples should be considered outliers. The rest of us fall somewhere in between.

Regarding long runs, some advise that the duration be prescribed by time versus distance. Famed running coach Dr. Jack Daniels advises that for recreational runners (nonelite), the long run should be no longer than 2:30. His theory is that because of the lack of adaptation on the body from a musculoskeletal point of view, running over 2:30 would increase the chance for injury to the point where it's not worth the gamble (757). Moreover, Dr. Daniels states that even if an individual's goal time is 4:30 and the person's longest run is 2:30, this volume disparity should not be cause for alarm, as it is not a necessity to train at or close to the race distance (757). In other words, Dr. Daniels looks at the long run from the perspective of stress on the body. For example, if a top-level marathoner runs the marathon in 2:20 whereas a recreational runner does it in 4:40, from purely a time perspective, the recreational runner places twice the amount of stress on the body as the top-level marathoner.

If prescribing long-run volume by time, the exact maximum should be determined by many factors, including training history, comparison between prescribed long-run time, and estimated marathon finish time and propensity for injury.

#### ARGUMENT AGAINST USING TIME-BASED DISTANCES

While nothing is inherently incorrect about prescribing a time-based long run, if the prescription is substantially below what a runner expects to run during the race, this likely poses several issues.

**Issue #1:**
If a time-based long run is prescribed because you are worried that the client cannot handle the standard long-run distance without a substantial chance of getting injured, what does this say about the person's overall readiness for the marathon?

Remember, the first goal of the runner is to finish the marathon, irrespective of a target finishing time. If the time covered in training is drastically less (over half) than what is expected on race day, a client is venturing into unknown territory as the person doesn't know how his or her body (and mind) will respond to the increase in time/miles. It may respond fine … but it may not.

**Issue #2:**
Running a marathon has as much to do with the mental aspect as it does the physical aspect. Let's say that a runner performs a long run of 2:30 but projects the marathon finish time to be five-plus hours. Even if the client could handle this increase in distance without physical injury, would the person be able to mentally handle the increased time on his or her feet? Likely not.

**Summary:**
Because of both the physical and mental factors involved with training for a marathon and specifically the long run, UESCA recommends a long-run prescription range of 18–22 miles. If a time-based long run is prescribed, it should roughly equate to the time required to run within the 18-to-22-mile range.

## FACTORS THAT INFLUENCE VOLUME INCREASES

There is little argument that substantial increases in training volume (both long run and weekly volume) from week-to-week will likely increase the chance for injury. What qualifies as a *substantial increase* is subjective, as it is based on the individual. Many factors influence volume increases within a training program. Following are the most influential:

- **New Runners:** Musculoskeletal system has not adapted to the impact of running.
- **Low Training Volume:** Body has not adapted to high volume.
  - Individuals at a high training volume typically tolerate large volume increases better than those at low training volumes.
- **Physiological Adaptation:** The speed at which your client adapts to cardiovascular and musculoskeletal training will affect how fast the individual can progress. Of the two, musculoskeletal adaptation is more significant in respect to volume progression.
- **Biomechanics:** If your client has substantial postural or biomechanical deviations that would likely increase the chance for injury, volume increases should be made sparingly or not at all and only after consultation with a specialist.
- **History:** If your client is an experienced runner, you should note the individual's long-run volume increases during previous years and the results (e.g., performance, injury, etc.) to help estimate the appropriate volume increases. Additionally, if there were any reasons for delays in the client's program progression such as injury or burnout, this should also be noted.
- **Injury:** If your client is recovering from an injury or sickness, initial volume increases, if any, should be minimal.
- **Current Training Volume:** Increases in volume are largely dependent on the current training volume of the client.
- **Genetics:** Some clients are naturally gifted in the area of endurance and musculoskeletal adaptation. However, until you work with a client for a substantial amount of time, you will not be able to determine this.
- **Ability to Recover:** Many factors affect one's ability to recover, including proper rest, nutrition, manageable volume, and intensity increases.

> The date or distance of an event is not a factor in increasing volume. Volume should never be increased to accommodate an event. If based on current training volume and proper volume progression a client will not be ready for an event, a new event must be selected.

## THE 10 PERCENT RULE (Fact or Fiction?)

A 10 percent maximum increase in volume is commonly recommended for running programs. Most coaches reference and use the 10 percent rule to steer their clients away from overtraining and potential injury. So where did this popular guideline originate from? No one knows. According to Carl Foster, director of the Human Performance Laboratory at the University of Wisconsin–La Crosse, the origin is "lost in history," and he notes that "whether it (the 10 percent rule) is right is undocumented" (130).

> **Challenge to the 10 Percent Volume Guideline**
>
> The genesis of the 10 percent volume increase rule is to reduce the risk for injury. A 2008 study out of the University of Groningen in the Netherlands challenges this notion. There were 532 subjects in this study, split into two groups to train for a four-mile running race (130). One group of runners was put on a 13-week program with 10 percent weekly increases in volume. The other group was put on an eight-week program with 50 percent weekly increases in volume. The study found that the 10 percent increase group had a 20.8 percent injury rate, whereas the 50 percent increase group had a 20.3 percent injury rate. In other words, the two groups essentially had the same injury rate, approximately one in four.
>
> While this study seems to indicate that the rate of volume increase is not a factor for injury, it is important to note that many factors contribute to injury, and thus, volume increases must be based on the individual.
>
> A 2012 study by Nielsen et al. also turned up similar findings (316). In this study, 60 novice runners tracked their distance over 10 weeks using GPS technology. Of the 60 runners, 13 sustained an injury. Those injured increased their weekly training volume 31.6 percent in comparison with those who were not injured (22.1 percent). While this study seems to demonstrate that there is an increased chance for injury with large increases in weekly training volume, it needs to be noted that the uninjured individuals increased their weekly training volume 22.1 percent, which is much greater than the customary 10 percent.

Dr. Ron Diercks from the University of Groningen in the Netherlands, who was a member of the team that conducted the study mentioned above, noted that in regard to the 10 percent rule, "Nobody found out if it works or what is the basis of it." Dr. Diercks goes on to say, "People hear something, they read something, and then it's like a religion" (130).

Given the lack of an origin or scientific explanation of the 10 percent rule, it appears to be largely based in running and training lore.

When using a set percentage (10 percent) for the purpose of volume increases, the initial increases will be quite small (assuming the client is starting out at a low training volume), and as the volume increases, the increases get larger and larger. There are two primary issues with this approach:

1. Increases in volume are based on a preset, arbitrary percentage, and therefore do not take into consideration an individual's adaptation response to training.
2. If a client were starting at a very low training volume, the initial increases would be so small that they would be deemed inconsequential.
   - For example, if your client is starting out running one mile, a 10 percent increase is .1 mile. Based on the 10 percent increase logic, the client would run 1.1 miles the following week.

> Clients respond differently to the same volume increases.

While this section debates the validity of the 10 percent rule, it must be noted that weekly increases of less than 10 percent (or no weekly increases) are standard in training programs.

### WEEKLY VOLUME INCREASES

Weekly volume increases are distributed unevenly across a week's worth of training. For example, if a client is running 40 miles per week, a 10 percent increase equates to a four-mile weekly increase for a total of 44 miles. Below is how the four miles might be distributed:

- **Long run:** 1.5 miles (13 to 14.5 miles)
- **Tempo run:** 1 mile (5 to 6 miles)
- **Easy run:** 1 mile (4 to 5 miles)
- **800-meter repeats:** .5 mile (one more repeat)

As the four additional miles are spread unevenly across four days (workouts), when you distribute the increase across X number of days, the daily increases are likely not substantial. While large weekly increases in volume may or may not increase the chance for injury, when discussing percentage volume increases, it would be advantageous to focus on daily volume increases, as this likely has a stronger correlation to injury rate than weekly volume increases.

Weekly volume increases can also be accommodated via an additional day(s) of running.

## PROGRAM CREATION OVERVIEW

On the surface, creating a training program is a fairly straightforward and intuitive process. However, with so many variables, it is often difficult to know exactly where to start and how to progress the program. As you never know who will want your coaching services, you cannot afford to make any assumptions (i.e., currently running 15 miles/week) when creating a training program. While it is fine to use some form of template for your clients, the template must be completely customizable to the needs of each client and make no assumptions.

Following is the step-by-step process by which UESCA recommends creating a training program. Each step is elaborated on later in the module.

Before you can take on clients, you *must* make sure that based on their desired goal(s), their current training level, and their availability to train, they will be well prepared for their event(s). If after taking these variables into account, you find that they will not be ready, you must either select a more reasonable goal or refuse to work with them.

Program creation comprises two areas:

1. **Feasibility mock-up**
   - Takes into account the distance of the goal event, client's current fitness level/training volume, and weekly training availability
2. **Full training program build-out (once the program is deemed feasible)**
   - Includes all aspects of the training program

Creating an effective training program is a dynamic process that is constantly changing. While it would be nice if the training program creation process would follow an easy, linear progression, the reality is that this process is full of zigs and zags and stops and starts and must take a near infinite number of variables into consideration.

While training program design products and services exist on the market that are more or less plug-and-play, you must realize and understood that if you can enter in just a few fields of information and can get a fully "customized" program, there is no way it can take all of the necessary variables into consideration. As a result, the program is likely lacking in one or more areas. The true value of quality coaches is that they should always have their eye on the client in respect to the training program and make any and all modifications to the program as needed.

### NOTHING IS 'STANDARD'

*Why does one client have two weeks at the same training volume before an increase while another client has three weeks at the same volume before an increase?*

*Why does one client have a peak training volume of 60 miles/week while another client has a peak volume of only 40 miles/week?*

These examples illustrate that when it comes to program design, there is no "standard." It is important to create a properly constructed training program versus using generic templates. This process takes longer and requires more education on the part of the coach, but it is the only way to ensure that a training program is correct and safe.

## PROGRAM CREATION PROCESS

Before you can build out a program for a client, you need to make sure that the event selected to focus on is feasible based on the following factors. Therefore these feasibility steps also represent the first four steps of the program design. The four steps to determine feasibility are:

1. Determine Training Availability
2. Identify Starting Volume (Long Run)
3. Plot Goals
4. Mock Up Long-Run Training Volume

If an event is deemed feasible based on these four steps, continue building out the program with step 5.

### STEP 1: TRAINING AVAILABILITY

While steps 1–4 are more or less assessed simultaneously, for the purpose of the certification, they are noted as singular steps.

Before you can start working with clients, you need to know how often they can train and for how long. In order to progress, their training availability must be appropriate for the event type/distance they are training for.

It is rare that a client will have the same availability each week. However, for most clients, there is a general range that they can approximate to train weekly.

If a client's schedule is such that they know exactly which days and times they are available to train, all the better. In this first step, it is a good idea to write down the client's actual time availability and with as much detail as possible. It might look something like this:

| Mon | Tues | Wed | Thu | Fri | Sat | Sun |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| No Availability | 30 Min Morning | 1 Hour Evening | No Availability | 30 Min Lunchtime | 1 Hour Morning | 30 Min Morning |

**Figure 14.3** Sample client availability schedule

In regard to misaligned goals, if the client whose time availability listed above has a goal to participate in a marathon, you must explain why the training availability is not sufficient for the goal and recommend that a 10K distance or less would be a better and more feasible option.

It is better to err on the side of caution when selecting an event based on one's training availability. For example, if your client thinks his or her schedule will open up down the road and enable the person to target a half marathon, it is better to go for the sure bet and target a 10K.

**RED FLAG!**
If a client's goal and training availability are grossly mismatched, such as having three days/week (one hour per day) to train for a marathon, you must inform the person this will not work and select a more applicable goal (i.e., 5–10K).

#### Realistic Programming

Clients have many obligations other than training, some of which come up unexpectedly. This is the norm, not the exception! It is your responsibility as the coach to develop a program that takes this into consideration, while keeping the client on track to reach his or her goal. For example, if your client has business meetings that come up often at the end of the day, it is advisable not to schedule critical workouts for that time. You need to work with your client as much as possible to accommodate the person's schedule and personal obligations.

However, clients can aim to participate in an event only if they have the time to train for it.

### STEP 2: INITIAL TRAINING VOLUME (Long Run)

Once you know that clients' training availability is appropriate for the type/distance event they are training for, you need to know if they are currently training and if so, what their longest run is. #### Currently Not Running

If a client is deconditioned and/or not currently running, you must be very careful to start the client at a low level and progress the individual slowly to allow the body to adapt from a musculoskeletal standpoint. If a client has never run before and has poor running form, a large focus must be on proper running form/gait. The initial training volume for a client such as this would likely be:

- Run/Walk–20 minutes

A run/walk program has an individual alternate between running and walking at set time intervals (i.e., walk five minutes, run five minutes). As a client progresses though the run/walk program, the run portion becomes longer and the walk portion becomes shorter.

Keep in mind that even if your client is in good aerobic shape, unless the person has been running recently, his or her muscles and connective tissue need time to adapt. This type of client should adhere closely to the preceding volume guideline.

This is also relevant to clients who trained and competed in running events the prior year and stayed in shape during the off-season but have not run recently. Assuming it has been at least one or two months since a client ran, the person should start out at or close to above the initial training guideline. These clients typically adapt faster to running than someone new to the sport, yielding a high rate of progression.

Once a client is up to an hour run/walk program, the individual can advance to a mileage-based (versus time-based) program. The starting run mileage should start out at two miles.

#### Tune-Up Races

Most runners who select a goal race will also select one or more tune-up (preparation) races. These races are important for many reasons: check fitness level, practice fueling, get used to race-day jitters, strategy/pacing, and a lot more. Runners typically do not taper, or do not taper to the same extent for tune-up races as they would for a goal event. As a result, most runners are not completely recovered for tune-up races. Many runners call this lack of a taper *training through a race*.

While a taper is typically not implemented for tune-up races, a runner should still decrease intensity prior to one. The last hard workout prior to a tune-up race should be two to five days in advance (720). The longer the tune-up race, the longer the duration between a hard workout and the tune-up race should be.

Athletes often run races at a casual pace, or at least well below race pace. This is often the case as runners sometimes choose to do a race versus a long run by themselves. It is typically easier for runners to motivate themselves to run a long distance if they have 5,000 of their closest friends with them. However, it should be noted that when a tune-up race is noted, it means running the race at race pace. A tune-up race is about a lot more than just gaining fitness or getting in a long run.

> **Trialing the Fueling Strategy**
>
> Tune-up races are a great time to practice a fueling strategy. While this can and should be done during training runs as well, everything from prerace, during the race, and post-race can be practiced with all of the dynamics and intricacies of race day! For example, a "nervous" stomach may react differently to the same fuel used in training and on race day.

If an event has a course map but no elevation profile, you can use the website *MapMyRun.com* to create the race map. Clicking on the elevation feature will create the elevation map. This information should not only help shape the overall training process, but the selection of the tune-up race(s) as well.

### STEP 4: THE LONGEST RUN

This step is what completes the feasibility aspect of the program. The two elements of this step that are the most important to be aware of are:

1. **Longest run**
2. **Suggested long run training volume increases**

#### LONGEST RUN

The longest run is used to benchmark a client's preparedness in respect to a goal event. The reasoning behind this is simple – having the ability to run at or near the full distance of the goal event is a prerequisite to being able to compete and finish a race/event feeling good and with the least chance for injury.

For marathons, peak mileage (long run) is typically attained twice during the program – once during the week before the first taper week and the other three weeks before the first taper week (there is typically a low-volume "long run" in the week between these two peak-mileage long runs).

The typical mileage range for the longest run is:

- **Half Marathon:** 12–13 miles
- **Marathon:** 18–22 miles
- **10K:** 6–10 miles

#### Why Not Benchmark Weekly Training Volume?

While long runs are a necessity in a training program buildup, training volume (running) may be substituted with other forms of cross-training. As a result, it is possible to perform well at an event with a relatively low overall running volume so long as the long run progression is present in the schedule.

#### When Do Long Runs Begin?

As noted in the beginning of this module, most training programs assume that an individual is currently running a certain number of miles per week and has also attained a particular mileage for the longest run. Following are typical training program long-run mileage prerequisites in relation to the duration of the training program. This correlates with when the long runs begin.

| Event | Current Longest Run | Program Duration |
|---|---|---|
| Marathon | 6 miles | 16–20 weeks* |
| Half Marathon | 4 miles | 10–12 weeks* |

*The program duration includes the taper weeks

Using these guidelines, if a client is not yet up to the long-run mileage noted, the program will be longer than the noted program duration.

One day per week is typically designated as the "long run" day. Most programs designate the same day during the week for the long run. However, this is not a requirement. Therefore, when designing a program, any day of the week can be allocated as the "long run" day.

Initially the "long run" may be one mile – however, this will build as the client adapts to the program. Therefore in regard to the question, "When do long runs begin?" the answer is immediately.

#### Over-Distance Training

This is characterized by training at a longer distance than the race distance. This type of training is most prevalent with 5K and 10K events. Over-distance training should not be part of a marathon program. If the goal event is a half marathon, there is no reason for over-distance training. However, if a half marathon is being used as a tune-up race for a marathon, there will be training days at a greater distance than 13.1 miles. Following are several reasons why over-distance training should be left out of marathon programs:

- **Time:** As it already takes a lot of time to train for a marathon, over-distance training will take time away from other aspects of training, and more important, it will take time away from other aspects of a client's life (e.g., family time).
- **Injury:** Generally speaking, the longer an individual runs, the greater the chance for an overuse injury. Therefore, even if there is a slight fitness advantage, which is debatable, this advantage would likely be offset by an increased chance for injury.
- **Catch Up:** Training for a marathon is not like a school exam: You cannot cram for it. If the race date is near and a client is unprepared from a fitness or endurance perspective, it is better to pass on the event and choose a shorter event or select a marathon further out.
- **No Need:** Above all else, there is no need to train at a further distance than a marathon.
  - One exception is if your client is training for an ultra-marathon. This particular race distance(s) is not largely focused on in this certification.

## SAMPLE PROGRAM SCENARIOS

Clients will come to you with unique situations and goals. You will need to be able to assess each client individually to devise an appropriate and effective training program.

Following are examples of types of clients whom you might come across – all of whom require drastically different programs:

### SCENARIO ONE

**A deconditioned and overweight client who wants to lose weight and whose motivation is to participate in a 5K at the end of the year.**

The first step would be to connect the client with a registered dietitian for assistance in creating a nutritional strategy that will enable the person to lose weight, while still having enough energy to train properly. Depending on height/weight, you might need to be sensitive to the impact of running on the body. This program would more closely resemble an overall fitness/weight-loss routine with a slant toward running-specific training.

### SCENARIO TWO

**An experienced runner who is trying to qualify for the Boston Marathon.**

You need to find out exactly what the individual's strengths and weaknesses are to get the client to the next level. This would involve biomechanical assessments, weekly training program adjustments, nutritional strategies (in conjunction with an RD), and perhaps much more. Working with this type of client will require all of your expertise and, most likely, referrals to other specialists.

If you do not feel comfortable coaching an experienced client, it is best to refer the person to someone else. Just because you are a great coach to one particular level of runner does not necessarily mean you are a great coach to other levels. This is not a weakness, but rather a function of reality; you cannot be everything to everyone. Good coaches know their strengths and weaknesses, and their clientele reflects this.

### SCENARIO THREE

**Client who works 80 hours a week and has family obligations signed up for a marathon and now needs to find a way to train properly, while not taking time or energy away from work or family.**

This type of client is always a challenge to work with because of time constraints, and more than likely, you will not be able to prepare the individual perfectly for the event. It is your job to work with what you have and to prepare the client as best as possible given the circumstances. In this case, it might mean advising the client against doing the goal event and shifting focus to a shorter distance. Important things to ascertain initially are starting fitness level, when the race is, and how much time the person has per week to commit to training. If the date of the event does not align with the amount of training time available, you should recommend a shorter event to focus on.

Clients must be accountable. Just because you prescribe a workout does not necessarily mean a client will follow it. This is not always your client's fault. For example, you might have prescribed an evening training session but maybe a child got sick or a business dinner popped up and the person is unable to do the training. This is life, and you will need to adjust the training program accordingly. However, if these things become habitual or if a client just doesn't follow the training program, you need to make it clear that the lack of preparation is not your fault but rather due to the person's lack of adherence to the program.

### SCENARIO FOUR

**A person who is relatively fit wants to do a 10K to stay motivated and to have a goal.**

This type of client is quite common. The emphasis of working with this type of client is overall fitness with a slant toward running. Remember, this is not someone whose goal in life is to become an elite runner. The longer the distance of the running race being targeted, the more specialized the focus on running must be.

## CLIENT CASE STUDY

**SUBJECT:** 40-year-old male runner ("Mike")

**DATE:** April 1, 2015

**EXPERIENCE:** Has been running for the past four years. In the last two years, he has run a half marathon each year in 2:20 and 2:15, respectively.

**GOAL:** Run a half marathon in 1:59 or less and lose 10 pounds.

**HEALTH HISTORY:** Cleared by physician to run and no cardiovascular issues. Clear HHQ and PARQ. No medications. Has had past issues with hamstring tendinosis (left biceps femoris), neck tightness, and groin pain (bilateral).

**TIMELINE:** Goal event is five months from today (August 1, 2015).

**TRAINING AVAILABILITY:** During week, one hour in the morning. On weekends, up to three hours on Saturday and Sunday.

**CURRENT TRAINING VOLUME:** 15 miles/week (six miles longest run) plus two hours of yoga/week.

**ASSESSMENT RESULTS:**

- Height/Weight: 5'11", 175 lbs
- Body Fat (calipers): 21%
- Perceived Intensity: High pain tolerance
- Resting Heart Rate: 62 bpm
- Maximum Aerobic Heart Rate: 173 bpm
- MLSS (30-minute time trial): 159
- Overhead Squat Screen:
  - Knees moved inward
  - POSSIBLE ISSUE: Weak glute medius (hip abductors), tight hip adductors (could be the cause of groin pain if the adductors are strained)

### UESCA CLIENT PROFILE

1. What type of physical activities do you currently participate in (i.e. sports, weight lifting, etc.)? Running, cycling, yoga
2. What competitive sports (if any) have you participated in? Running
3. Why, when, and how did you get started in running? 4 years ago
4. Will you be training by yourself or with others? By myself
5. What are your short- and long-term running goals? Run a sub 2-hr half marathon. Lose 10 lbs. Both goals are 5 months from today.
6. When do you have the most energy, morning or evening? Morning
7. Does your schedule vary or is it fairly consistent? Consistent
8. What days/times do you have to train? Week–mornings (1 hr), Weekends–day (up to 3 hrs each day)
9. How important is structure in your life? Very important
10. What aspect of running are you most intimidated by (if any)? Running too fast and hitting the wall. I have pacing issues.
11. Name your top three running-related strengths and weaknesses.
    - Strengths: Endurance, high pain tolerance, no orthopedic injuries
    - Weaknesses: Bad at pacing, eat too much, poor running gait
12. Do you respond better to tough love, handholding, or somewhere in between? Somewhere in between
13. Do you crack, stay the same, or thrive under pressure? Thrive
14. What are you better at? Short, hard sprints and intervals or long endurance efforts? Long endurance efforts
15. How competitive of a person are you? Rate yourself from 1–10 (10 being the most): 7
16. How much do you enjoy the "pain" of pushing hard in a race/training? 1–10: 9
17. How confident are you in your abilities as a runner? 1–10: 5
18. You like to be challenged 1–10 (1=untrue, 10=very true): 10
19. Look forward to hard training sessions 1–10 (1=untrue, 10=very true): 8
20. Susceptible to mental burnout 1–10 (1=untrue, 10=very true): 3

### SUMMARY/NOTES

Mike has a moderate amount of running experience. As the majority of one's energy while running goes to supporting the body, an emphasis on weight loss will result in Mike becoming a faster runner – in addition to other aspects of training.

His issue with pacing is that he pushes past his limit and often hits the wall. This is compounded by his high tolerance for pain. Therefore an emphasis must be placed on adhering to pacing guidelines. A GPS watch that has a real-time pace function is recommended so that he can "feel" a particular pace and see how that correlates to the actual pace.

He has very good endurance and low risk for mental burnout and is very disciplined when it comes to workouts, so there will not be much of an issue with program adherence, especially in regard to long runs. However, because he loves long runs, he often avoids short, hard efforts in favor of long, slow runs. To hit his goal time, short, structured, and intense workouts need to be added to his program.

He has a very long gait – especially in front of his body. As a result, his biceps femoris issue may be due to the large eccentric strain placed on the hamstrings to decelerate the lower leg. Shortening this aspect of the stride and possibly converting to a midfoot strike may help.

Since weight loss is a noted goal, referring him to a registered dietician is advised.

**Areas of Focus:**

- Shortening the stride, possible midfoot conversion
- Refer to RD – weight loss
- Fewer long runs and more speedwork
- Focus on adhering to structured pacing
- Increase range of motion of hip adductors and strengthen hip abductors
- Maintain yoga twice per week

**Recovery:** 87–105 bpm
**Aerobic Base:** 106–123 bpm
**Aerobic Development:** 124–141 bpm
**Threshold:** 142–159 bpm
**Supra Threshold:** 160–166 bpm
**Max Aerobic:** 167–173 bpm

## SUMMARY

- Preformatted training programs should not be used for clients as they lack customization and, therefore, ignore many training variables.
  - Preformatted training programs are a good place to get ideas, but they should not be prescribed to a client without modifications.
- The steps noted in this section relate to the fastest a client could be safely progressed to reach peak weekly volume. Therefore, a slower progression is likely prescribed.
- UESCA recommends a training program that comprises the following three components:
  - Full "At a Glance" program
  - Weekly training programs
  - Workout database
- Many factors affect the degree and frequency of volume increases:
  - Biomechanics
  - Historical training data
  - Injury
  - Current training volume
  - Genetics
- The 10 percent maximum volume increase rule is largely erroneous.
- While maximum individual-session volume increases are noted in this section, smaller volume increases are likely to occur and are based on the individual client.
- Over distance training relates to training at a longer distance than the race distance.
- There are seven main steps to create the training program:
  1. Training Availability
  2. Initial Training Volume (long run)
  3. Plot Goals
  4. The Longest Run
  5. Integrating Weekly Volume and Structure
  6. Development of Daily/Weekly Schedule
  7. Putting It All Together
