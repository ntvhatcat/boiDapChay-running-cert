# Module 7: Running Mechanics

Running is an impact sport and therefore tends to have a relatively high injury rate in relation to other endurance sports. Because of this, a runner's form and, more specifically, the running gait are often analyzed for the purpose of improving efficiency and preventing injury.

Unless you are a physical therapist, chiropractor, or physician, you cannot diagnose or treat a suspected injury. Biomechanical assessments are the first place to start when evaluating your client. The next phase is to assess clients while they are running.

## Running Economy

The mechanics, and therefore the form of a runner, is often termed *running economy*. Many factors influence running economy, such as calf muscle girth, bone length, running form, and stride rate. Running economy relates to how efficient a runner is. A study found that the variation in efficiency between well-trained runners with good running economy and untrained runners with poor running economy is between 5 and 7 percent (715).

Just because an individual runs doesn't mean the person is efficient at it. **Running is a learned skill**, just like other sports (731).

## Terminology

**Pronation:** Inward (medial) rotation of the foot. Normal during a proper gait pattern; helps absorb stress from impact.
- **Overpronation:** Excessive pronation

**Supination (under pronation):** Occurs when the foot "rolls" laterally. Essentially a lack of pronation. During supination, the subtalar joint does not flex.

**Heel-to-Toe Drop:** Difference in height between the heel and forefoot of a shoe.

**Ground Reaction Force (GRF):** Based on Newton's third law, GRF is the force exerted by the ground on a body in contact with it (665).

**Spinal Engine:** Developed by Dr. Serge Gracovetsky. Counter-rotation of the spine results in transverse hip/pelvic rotation and is a major influence in the gait cycle. Upon foot strike while running, energy is not absorbed by the ground but rather utilized up the body's kinetic chain to assist with locomotion.

**Coupled Motion:** A sequence of movements by different body parts to create an efficient and powerful movement.

**Dorsiflexion:** Pointing of the foot upward (flexion of ankle joint).

**Plantar Flexion:** Pointing of the foot downward (extension of ankle joint).

**Free Energy:** Energy being stored (potential energy) via stretch of a muscle and/or connective tissue. By utilizing this free energy, the active muscle requirement is reduced while still producing the required amount of force.

**Stretch-Shortening Cycle (SSC):** Representative of an eccentric contraction of a muscle followed by a rapid concentric contraction of the same muscle. In running, the quadriceps, obliques, and calves are most influenced by the SSC.

**Passive Movement:** Body movement that is not the result of active muscle contractions. Lower leg (tibia/fibula) swing and hip flexion are influenced exclusively by passive movement.

**Pedestrian Gait Model:** The legs are the primary aspect of the body responsible for locomotion and the upper body is largely passive.

**Windlass Mechanism:** Prior to the foot landing when running, the toes are dorsiflexed, causing the plantar fascia to tighten, thus creating a stiff foot arch.

**Vertical Oscillation:** Vertical motion of a runner. While some vertical movement is normal, excessive vertical oscillation reduces efficiency.

**Torsion:** Twisting of an object from one end relative to the other end.

**Aponeurosis:** Tendon that is typically flat and broad (sheet-like). Plantar fascia is an example.

## Gait Phases

Running gait (cycle) has three phases:

1. **Recovery (Swing):** Any time a foot is in the air.
2. **Drive:** Starts when the leg is slightly behind the runner's hips and continues until right before the foot leaves the ground.
3. **Support:** Starts when the foot hits the ground until the leg is directly underneath the hips. With a correct midfoot strike, the support phase is either eliminated or substantially minimized.

---

## Ground Reaction Force (GRF)

GRF relates to Newton's third law: *For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction.*

In running, each foot strike exerts a force **2.5 times one's bodyweight**. This force is transmitted through your foot and up the leg.

A study by Keller et al. found that vertical GRF was 50 percent higher during slow jogging than walking or running, associated with greater vertical oscillation (666). The higher the GRF, the greater the chance for injury (667).

## Pronation / Supination

### Pronation and Supination

Three common designations of foot movement throughout the gait cycle:

1. **Mild Pronation**
2. **Overpronation**
3. **Supination (underpronation)**

### Role of the Big Toe

During running, the big toe (first metatarsophalangeal joint) plays a key role in:

- Stabilizing the foot
- Regulating the degree of foot pronation
- Forward propulsion

**First Metatarsophalangeal Joint Facts:**
- Carries 12 times more weight than the small toe
- Only toe made up of two bones as opposed to three
- Has a separate set of control muscles and tendon insertions

The **windlass mechanism** of the foot pertains to the lifting (dorsiflexion) of the toes. Prior to the foot landing, the toes are dorsiflexed, causing the plantar fascia to tighten, creating a stiff foot arch. Upon landing, the toes plantar flex and the arch flattens (shock absorption). When the leg moves into the drive phase, the toes begin to dorsi-flex, turning the arch into a rigid lever for push-off.

### Shoe Implications

Running shoes that are too narrow crowd the toes in the toe box, negatively affecting the roles of the big toe and reducing efficiency. It is advised to run in shoes that have a **wide enough toe box**. Chronically wearing footwear with a crowded toe box can cause a bunion.

### Shoe-Wear Patterns

The wear pattern on the sole tells you about foot strike:
- Very little or no heel wear → midfoot or forefoot strike
- **Neutral:** even wear across the heel and forefoot
- **Pronation:** medial (inside) heel and forefoot wear
- **Supination:** lateral (outside) wear

### Mild Pronation

Pronation helps the body absorb stress from the impact of running and walking. Four primary phases of normal gait in regard to foot pronation:

1. Foot strikes the ground: slightly supinated (land on outside heel)
2. Foot travels through the support phase: transitions from supinated to pronated
3. Foot begins the drive phase: in a pronated position
4. Foot prepares to exit drive phase: begins to supinate; big toe plays a large role

### Overpronation

Many clinicians believe that a subtalar joint angle greater than 8–10 degrees equates to overpronation. Overpronation is associated with increased ground reactive forces and foot-contact time (664).

- Custom orthotics or over-the-counter orthotics may help
- Motion-control running shoes are often recommended
- A 2008 study by Cheung et al. found that degree of pronation tended to increase with distance ran (68)
- Weak hip abductor muscles (gluteus medius) can also contribute

### Supination

Supination (also called underpronation) is essentially a lack of pronation. Those who supinate typically have a high and rigid foot arch and run on the lateral aspect of their feet.

> **What Are Flat Feet?** Flat feet (*pes planus*) = little to no arch in the standing position. A study by Menz (1998) found that static assessments to determine arch height classifications were unreliable (610). Orthotics and arch-strengthening exercises are often prescribed.

### Assessing Pronation and Supination

**Visual Signs of Overpronation:**
- Knock-knees
- During recovery phase, lower leg swings excessively outward
- Substantial inward angle of the ankle

**Visual Signs of Supination:**
- Bowlegged
- During recovery phase, lower leg swings excessively medially
- Outward angle of the ankle

### Pronation Is Not Indicative of Pain or Injury

It is a popular belief that pronation is bad and will likely lead to injury. However, pronation is a natural occurrence that acts to absorb stress, transfer load on the feet, provide elastic energy, and facilitate a stable platform for push-off. Study after study has proved that pronation is not indicative of pain or injury (424, 425, 426). Several studies theorize that running injuries stem more from training errors (429) and hip dysfunction (430).

## Q Angle

The Q angle is assessed by measuring the angle between two lines (57):
- **Red Line:** ASIS to the middle of the patella
- **Blue Line:** Middle of patella to the tibial tubercle

Women typically have a greater Q angle than men because of a wider pelvis. The normal Q angle range is 15–22 degrees. Recent research has shown there is **not a strong correlation** between an individual's Q angle and the incidence of PFS (646).

### Knee Valgus

Excessive medial knee displacement (EMKD) — knee moving medially. Potential causes:

- Large Q angle
- Foot overpronation
- Overactive hip adductors
- Underactive hip abductors – primarily the gluteus medius
- Lack of ankle dorsiflexion

**Ankle Dorsiflexion:** Individuals with poor ankle dorsiflexion during foot impact had greater ground reaction forces associated with EMKD (780). Ankle dorsiflexion is essentially a shock absorber.

## Running Form Components

Following are **11 running-form principles** based on biomechanics and running economy.

> **Energy Allocation** — The majority of energy goes to supporting body weight, followed by forward propulsion (drive phase) and swinging the recovery leg forward (585). Arm swing and balance have a minimal metabolic cost (586).

### 1 – Look Straight Ahead, Not Down or Up

Looking down causes shoulders to roll anteriorly and rotate internally, leading to excessive spinal flexion. Looking upward causes the lower back to extend and the feet to strike substantially in front of the body.

### 2 – Upper Body Erect, Slight Forward Lean

A correct slight forward lean comes from the **ankles**, not the pelvis. From the heel to the shoulders, the body should have a slight forward lean.

**Center of Mass (COM):** 73 percent of a runner's total energy expenditure is used to support body weight; only approximately 20 percent is used for forward propulsion (730). The quadriceps and foot plantarflexors (gastrocnemius, soleus) are the primary contributors to accelerating the body's COM during running (695).

### 3 – No High (i.e., Bounce) or Long Stride

A stride that pushes runners upward wastes energy. The brim of a hat should not rise or fall substantially.

**Vertical Oscillation:** Excessive vertical oscillation is inefficient. The more vertical oscillation, the more energy must be expended to counteract gravity.

### 4 – Foot Strikes the Ground Slightly in Front of or under the Body

Two primary reasons:

1. **Hamstring Stress:** Overstriding puts substantial eccentric stress on the hamstrings
2. **Direct Power Transmission:** When the foot strikes the ground under the body, it immediately pushes down and rearward, propelling the runner forward

### 5 – Rotation of Pelvis (Hips)

It is natural for the pelvis to rotate when walking/running. Pelvic rotation helps minimize the impact of running — by the time the foot strikes the ground, that side of the pelvis is already rotating posteriorly, which reduces impact on the body (224).

**Assessment:** If your client's torso rotates 45 degrees or more (noted at the shoulders) in the frontal plane, there is likely too much pelvic rotation. 15–30 degrees of upper-body rotation typically equates to the appropriate amount.

### 6 – Sweep, Don't Pound

When the foot strikes the ground, it should already be moving in a sweeping motion toward the rear of the body (like a cat "pawing" at the ground). A sweeping foot strike creates a fluid and powerful stride; a pounding foot strike creates a broken, nonfluid stride.

### 7 – Keep Hands, Shoulders, and Face Relaxed

When a runner's face is tightened, hands clenched, and shoulders shrugged, energy is diverted that should be going to the legs and core muscles.

### 8 – Arm Movement from Shoulders, Not Elbows

Arm movement should come primarily from the shoulders, not the elbows. Active flexion and extension of the elbows waste energy. Arm motion is used to counterbalance momentum of the legs (695). Swinging the arms (not excessively) saved a runner approximately **4 percent in energy cost** versus not swinging the arms (586).

**Arm Crossover:** Some degree of medial arm movement is suggested. Running with arms in a forward motion only puts excessive strain on external rotator cuff muscles of the shoulders.

### 9 – Short Front Stride with Fast Leg Turnover

Shortening of one's stride pertains to eliminating or greatly reducing the foot strike ahead of the body. The ideal stride rate and stride length function on a bell curve (573).

### 10 – Hip Extension

Hip extension (leg behind body) is an essential aspect of proper running form. Hip extension should come from the hip, not the lumbar spine. Proper hip extension is also a function of transverse pelvic rotation.

**Role of the 'Glutes':**
- The primary role of the GMax (gluteus maximus) is hip extension
- Most runners do not have the proper degree of hip extension because of a short posterior stride and/or little to no transverse hip rotation
- Too much vertical oscillation requires excessive GMax activation
- If runners lack hip flexor flexibility, they likely lack adequate hip extension

### 11 – Neutral Spine

Running with an anterior pelvic tilt (more common) results in runners with a substantial lumbar arch (lordosis). Tight hamstrings create excessive tension during the swing phase, increasing risk for hamstring injury.

## Assessing Running Form: Treadmill vs. Outdoors

A treadmill is a good tool to assess foot/ankle position and foot strike type. However, it has weaknesses when assessing overall form:

- When running outdoors, the foot pushes downward and rearward to propel forward
- On a treadmill, the belt sweeps the foot rearward
- Treadmill can artificially **induce pelvic rotation** not present when running on solid ground

**It is strongly advised to assess pelvic rotation and overall form while watching the person run outdoors at varying speeds.**

| | Benefits | Drawbacks |
|---|---|---|
| **Treadmill** | Easy to assess form; can manipulate speed/incline; widely available | Can induce artificial hip rotation; belt facilitates rearward foot movement |

Fatigue has been shown to alter the biomechanics of the lower extremities, specifically the knee and hips (337). The form at the start of a marathon is likely quite different from the form at the end.

### Self-Selection of Running Form

For new runners, the answer is definitely **no** — they do not self-select the most efficient form. A 2014 study by De Ruiter et al. found that when self-selecting running stride rate, no novice runners selected the most efficient rate (779). The more experienced runners are, the closer they run to their ideal SR. Simply by running more, runners can improve their running economy (778).

## Free Energy

Two areas of free energy:

1. **Energy Return**
2. **Passive Movement**

The goal of a runner is to optimize running form to convert as much stored (impact) energy as possible into forces that assist in forward movement.

Five areas of focus: hip rotation in the transverse plane, hip flexion, foot plantar flexion, knee flexion/extension, and foot-arch compression.

### Stretch-Shortening Cycle (SSC)

> *"Energy cannot be created or destroyed; it can only be changed from one form to another."* — Albert Einstein

The SSC is representative of an eccentric contraction of a muscle followed by a rapid concentric contraction of the same muscle. In running, the quadriceps, obliques, and calves are most influenced by the SSC. The stretch aspect of the muscle and tendon during the SSC is quite small — **6 to 7 percent** (615).

Variables that affect the degree of elastic return (525):
1. Length of the stretch
2. Speed of the stretch (loading)
3. Stiffness of the muscle and tendon
4. Time between the stretch and the contraction

From a running perspective, the legs act as springs. Springs compress during the first half of the support phase and rebound during the drive phase. **The stiffer a muscle is, the greater the amount of energy that can be stored and released.**

### Tendons

Tendons have elastic properties that allow them to stretch. The stretch stores energy; when unloaded, the stored energy is released.

The **elastic region** is the degree of stretch that elicits the ideal stretch reflex. Once the stretch extends past this region (the **plastic region**), the structure of the tendon changes and therefore changes the tendon length (522).

**Achilles Tendon:** A 16 percent increase in tricep surae tendon stiffness via resistance training decreased the rate of oxygen consumption during running by 4 percent, thereby increasing running economy (530).

- Shoes with a high heel-to-toe drop **reduce stored energy** (reduced ankle dorsiflexion places Achilles and calf in a shortened position)
- Insufficient Achilles tendon flexibility also limits potential energy stored

**Outside Influences:** The more solid the interface between foot and ground, the more efficient the runner will be. Soft surfaces (sand) or shoes with a lot of cushioning reduce the amount of energy that can be used.

**Trainability:** Sprint training stiffens the Achilles tendon, more efficiently utilizing elastic energy return.

### Foot Arch

The longitudinal arch acts as a spring to provide passive energy while running (601). The intrinsic foot muscles have the capacity to control deformation of the longitudinal arch (601). A common argument for minimalist footwear: since the arch is supported in conventional running shoes, the elastic energy of the longitudinal arch is negated.

### Muscles

Preactivation of leg muscles when running prepares the body for foot impact and is theorized to decrease stress to leg muscles. Leg muscle "stiffness" is controlled consciously and unconsciously (526). When running on different surfaces, the degree of leg stiffness will change to maintain the same degree of total vertical stiffness.

### Passive Movement

Passive movement is body movement that is **not the result of active muscle contractions**. Specific to running, these movements are influenced exclusively by passive movement:

- Lower leg (tibia/fibula) swing
- Hip flexion
- Knee flexion

The degree of passive movement is largely dependent on actions done prior to the passive movement occurring. The **lower leg swing** is the result of how extended the hip is at the end of the drive phase — the greater the hip extension, the larger the lower leg swing (think: pendulum).

Hip flexion during the leg-swing phase is a passive motion, requiring minimal muscle activation of the hip flexors.

**Training Implications:** Common drills like **butt kicks** and **high knees** take passive movements and turn them into active ones, requiring additional muscle activation. These drills may do more harm than good — there is no need to train a passive movement in isolation. Strides are a more beneficial drill.

## Allocation of Energy

Dr. Rodger Kram discovered the energy breakdown while running (585):

- **69 percent** — Supporting body weight
- **29 percent** — Forward propulsion

Taking muscle synergy into account, supporting body weight and forward propulsion equates to **98 percent of the energy** used by a runner (587).

- Swinging the arms reduced energy cost by approximately 4 percent (586)
- Balance required by a runner equated to 2 percent of energy cost
- The remaining aspects: running surface, shoes, and step width (587, 588)

## Rotational-Driven Running

While hip (pelvic) rotation in the transverse plane is an important aspect of the running gait, it is also one of the **least-discussed** aspects of running form. By leaving out pelvic rotation, a major aspect of force production is eliminated.

### The Spinal Engine

Developed by Dr. Serge Gracovetsky. The spinal engine model states that upon foot strike, energy is **not absorbed by the ground but rather utilized up the body's kinetic chain** to assist with locomotion (619).

This model consists of two primary aspects:
1. **Spinal Rotation**
2. **Coupled Motion**

**How the Pedestrian vs. Spinal Engine models interpret GRF:**
- **Pedestrian Model:** Energy is absorbed by the body
- **Spinal Engine:** Energy is utilized via counter-rotation of the spine to facilitate locomotion

**Spinal Rotation:** When running, the spine rotates (twists) in opposite directions — counter-rotation of the hips and torso. This counter-rotation involves the SSC to utilize potential energy for stabilizing the body and assisting locomotion via transverse rotation of the hips (774).

### Spiral Spring System

The intervertebral disks are not for shock absorption but rather to **store and unload energy via torsion** of the spine (775). The spine acts as a long spring. The twisting of the spring in opposing directions is representative of spinal rotation and thus transverse rotation of the hips and torso.

### Coupled Motion

Dr. Dalton modified Dr. Gracovetsky's spinal engine model to include the **anterior spiraling spring system (ASSS)** and the **posterior spiraling spring system (PSSS)** (776).

**ASSS muscles:**
- External Oblique
- Abdominal fascia and external oblique aponeurosis
- Contralateral Internal Oblique
- Contralateral Adductors

**PSSS muscles:**
- Latissimus Dorsi
- Fascia of the thoracic and lumbar spine region
- Contralateral Glute Max
- Contralateral Biceps Femoris

These spring systems gain and release energy via the SSC. They stretch and contract in a kinetic chain to facilitate spinal rotation and therefore transverse pelvic rotation.

**How the spring systems work:**
1. When hips and shoulders are counter-rotated the most → maximum potential energy stored; legs are farthest apart in the sagittal plane
2. As hips and shoulders rotate back toward each other → potential energy converts to kinetic energy
3. When hips and shoulders are in the same frontal plane → least tension on spring systems

### Arm Motion

Running with **no arm movement** takes away an element of the ASSS and reduces the total force of the spiral spring system. As the latissimus dorsi is primarily stretched by forward arm movement, increasing arm movement places a greater stretch on the lat, increasing the potential and kinetic energy of the PSSS. This is why:
- Track sprinters have very muscular upper bodies
- When you go from slow running to a sprint, arm movement becomes more pronounced

### Range of Motion

Too little rotation will not create enough stored (potential) energy; too much rotation will decrease running economy. The ideal amount exists on a bell curve. The speed of the runner affects the optimal degree of spinal rotation.

### Summary

> *Counter-rotation of the spine facilitates hip extension via hip rotation in the transverse plane. This allows for increased glute and core (primarily obliques) involvement and reduced ground reaction force (GRF). The result is less reliance on the hamstrings, reduced compression of the spine, and increased force production.*
>
> *Net effect = A faster and more efficient/economical runner due to a reduced metabolic cost of gait.*

### Relationship between Hip Rotation and Running Speed

The amount of hip rotation is typically proportional to the speed of a runner. The greatest amount of transverse hip rotation is often seen in middle-distance runners (400–1,600 meters) and sprinters.

At slow speeds, there is minimal transverse hip rotation, which increases reliance on leg muscles (especially hamstrings). At faster speeds, transverse hip rotation via the spinal engine becomes more important.

### Cueing Hip Rotation

**Cue Shoulders:** Instructing clients to increase or decrease rotation of the shoulders directly affects the rotation of the hips. To demonstrate: clasp hands together with arms straight ahead while running — this prevents torso rotation and greatly reduces hip rotation.

**Cue Leg/Hip Extension:** Instruct runners to focus on getting full leg/hip extension behind their body. Cueing clients to push down and back with a foot until full extension causes the hips to rotate to the side of the extended leg.

**Cue Arms:** Fore/aft arm (humerus) movement contributes to stretching the latissimus dorsi, part of the kinetic chain involved with the PSSS.

### Correlation between Ankle Flexibility and Hip Extension

When the leg is in the drive phase, the ankle must have adequate dorsiflexion or else the drive phase will be cut short — reducing hip extension. If your client has poor ankle flexibility, work on stretching the calf muscles (gastrocnemius and soleus) as well as utilizing a foam roller.

> **Shortening one's stride** should refer to reducing stride length *in front of* the body (overstriding), **not** behind it. The posterior stride length should not be shortened — in fact, for most runners, the posterior aspect of the drive phase is too short.

## Midfoot Strike

In recent years, the number of runners converting to a midfoot strike has increased dramatically, coinciding with an increase in minimalist running shoe usage. A catalyst was the book *Born to Run* by Christopher McDougall (69).

### Is a Midfoot Strike Right for Everybody?

**Probably not.** A heel strike puts more stress on the skeletal system whereas a midfoot strike puts more stress on the muscular system — especially the calves. Some clients, regardless of the length of adaptation, will have muscular/connective tissue issues when running with a midfoot strike. This must be ascertained on an individual basis.

### Midfoot Strike vs. Heel Strike

Biomechanically, a midfoot strike produces **less impact force** on the body than a heel strike (321). A heel strike is more reliant on the skeletal system for support and stability; a midfoot strike relies more on the musculature system.

If your client heel strikes and is not experiencing issues, the *"if it ain't broke don't fix it"* theory may be applied. However, if biomechanical or flexibility reasons suggest a midfoot strike would benefit the client, consider slowly converting.

**Running with a Heel Strike = Running with the Brakes On?** This is technically incorrect. The braking aspect can be more accurately described as *coasting* — during the support phase, the body uses momentum to continue forward movement until the leg is under the body and the drive phase can commence.

### Ground Contact during a Midfoot Strike: Balls of the Feet Only?

Many people who adopt a midfoot strike impact the ground only with the balls of their feet. The emphasis should be on the ball of the foot, but it should **not be the only point of contact**. Once the ball of the foot impacts the ground, immediately thereafter the rest of the foot should lightly impact the ground. When watching a runner with a proper midfoot strike, it should almost look as though the **entire length of the foot hits the ground at the same time.**

The only time when the ball of the foot should hit the ground exclusively is when running up a steep hill or sprinting — this is called a *forefoot strike.*

### Drills: Midfoot Form

**Uphill Running:** Have a client run up a gradual (2–5 percent grade) hill. Traits are almost identical to a midfoot strike:
- Slight forward upper-body lean (from the ankles, not the hips)
- Foot lands on midfoot versus heel
- Feet land under body

**Resistance Running:** Wrap a long theraband or rope around the client's waist while you hold the ends behind the person. Instruct the client to run forward against the resistance, leaning forward at the ankles.

### Converting to Midfoot Form

1. Allow the midfoot and heel to impact the ground almost simultaneously to reduce stress on the calf muscles
2. Perform exercises to strengthen the soleus and Achilles tendon (jumping rope, walking on balls of feet)
3. Right before the foot hits the ground, it should be moving rearward and the hips should rotate toward the side of the foot strike
4. Do not bounce excessively — bouncing causes calf muscles and Achilles tendon to work excessively
5. Gradually introduce midfoot striking into runs while wearing nonminimalist shoes; a good starting time frame is 15 seconds to a minute for midfoot running

Athletes who convert to barefoot or minimalist running with no progression are at an increased chance for injury.

### When Not to Make Changes

If a client has a race in the near future (anything less than **five months**), it is advisable to **not convert** the individual to a midfoot strike for the event.

### Natural Foot Gait

Generally, the faster or more uphill an individual runs, the farther forward on the foot they tend to run. A 2013 GWU study researched 38 habitually barefoot runners in northwest Kenya and found that most runners ran with a **heel strike**, contradicting the theory that barefoot running automatically converts gait to a midfoot strike (321).

### Foot-Contact Time

A 2007 study by Hasegawa et al. at the 15K point of a half marathon found (322):
- A higher percentage of faster runners ran with a midfoot strike than slower runners
- Faster runners had less foot contact time than slower runners

**Running speed vs. foot-strike type:**
- Slow → Heel Strike
- Medium → Midfoot Strike
- Fast → Front Foot Strike (Sprinting)

## It's All About the Tibia

Running-gait efficiency correlates directly to the **angle of the tibia in relation to the ground at the time of foot strike**. Upon foot strike, the tibia should be at or close to **90 degrees** in relation to flat ground.

A foot-strike (tibia) angle at or near 90 degrees allows forward propulsion to occur almost immediately as the foot is directly under, or close to being under, the body's center of gravity. If the foot strikes substantially in front of the body, the GRF creates an upward and rearward moment that does not facilitate forward motion (215).

If your client runs with a heel strike, the goal should be to **shorten their stride in front of their body** so the feet land under, or close to being under, the body's center of gravity.

**Drill:** Have a client stand on one leg with the knee behind the ankle, then try to push off to move the body forward. The client will find that until the knee is slightly ahead of the ankle, forward propulsion is not possible.

## Stride Rate and Length

The only way to increase running speed is to increase stride rate and/or stride length (568). Both function on a bell curve — too slow/fast or too short/long will negatively impact a runner.

### Correlation between Stride Rate and Stride Length

- Initial increases in speed rely more on an increase in stride length versus stride rate
- At higher speeds, speed increases are more dependent on increasing stride rate and slightly decreasing stride length (569)
- Variables such as leg length, height, and leg mass are **not** primary factors in determining preferred stride rate and length (570)
- Within an acceptable range, it is more energy efficient to lengthen the stride than to increase the stride rate (571)

### Stride Length

An individual's stride length is typically self-selected based on running speed. Too short of a stride minimizes the SSC. Overstriders store more potential energy but have a reduction in the ability to use it (575).

> **Step vs. Stride:**
> - **Step:** initial foot contact of one foot to the initial foot contact of the opposing foot
> - **Stride:** comprises two consecutive steps (therefore a stride = two steps)

The "perfect" stride length should be long enough to store an adequate amount of elastic energy and short enough to be able to utilize it. This typically relates to a **short anterior stride length** (foot hitting ground directly in front of or directly under the body) and a **relatively long posterior stride** to take advantage of proper hip extension and transverse rotation.

### Vertical Oscillation

The longer the stride, the greater the vertical oscillation. The primary determinant of vertical oscillation pertains to how the drive leg pushes against the ground. The **correct angle of force is downward and rearward** (diagonally rearward). When a runner pushes directly down into the ground, the body moves primarily upward and not forward.

Transverse rotation of the pelvis (hip rotation) assists in reducing vertical oscillation due to energy being used for forward versus vertical propulsion.

**Backpack Assessment:** Running with a lightly loaded loosely-worn backpack is a great way to assess vertical oscillation and torso rotation. If the pack moves up and down more than a few inches, the runner has too much vertical oscillation. If the pack does not sway from left to right but only moves vertically, the client likely does not have sufficient hip/torso rotation.

### Stride Rate

**Two things to consider:**
1. Running with a fast stride rate does not necessarily equate to having proper form or being efficient
2. What constitutes a "fast" SR is subjective and largely based on the speed of the runner

At the 1984 Olympics, coach Jack Daniels observed that all but one runner had a stride rate of **180 or more per minute** (576). This has become a benchmark for many runners. However:
- A runner who attains SR of 180 on a fast workout may be below 180 on a long run
- Running at an 11-minute/mile pace with SR of 180 equates to a stride length of only 32 inches — hardly efficient (577)

### Conclusions

Any changes in SL and/or SR must be done with a focus on proper form. As a coach, **do not become fixated on a set number** regarding the optimal SR and SL — these are influenced on an individual level.

Steve Magness's view: Focus on other elements of form (foot strike, hip rotation, upper-body position, etc.) and the SL and SR will change accordingly (577).

## Biomechanical Running Assessments

These assessments should be evaluated while standing (static) and running (dynamic).

### Knees

**Issue: Valgus knees (knock-knees)**
- **Potential Muscular/Structural Causes:** Underactive glute maximus/medius; overactive hip adductors
- **Potential Structural Issues:** Foot arch collapses (excessive pronation); excessive Q angle
- **Potential Equipment Issues:** Shoes lacking proper arch/heel support
- **Action:** STRETCH: hip adductors; STRENGTHEN: glute max/medius, intrinsic foot muscles, calf muscles

**Issue: Varus knees (bowlegged)**
- **Potential Structural Issues:** Foot supination; genu varum (structural deformity — typically requires surgery or bracing)

### Back

**Issue: Excessive lumbar/thoracic extension (excessive anterior pelvic tilt)**
- **Potential Causes:** Overactive erector spinae, hip flexors; underactive abdominals
- **Action:** STRETCH: erector spinae, mid/low trapezius, hip flexors; STRENGTHEN: rectus abdominis, hamstrings, glutes

**Issue: Excessive lumbar/thoracic flexion (excessive posterior pelvic tilt)**
- **Potential Causes:** Overactive abdominals; underactive erector spinae
- **Action:** STRETCH: rectus abdominis, hamstrings; STRENGTHEN: erector spinae

### Shoulders

**Issue: Shoulders elevate and internally rotate**
- **Potential Causes:** Overactive upper trapezius; underactive lower trapezius and rhomboids
- **Action:** STRETCH: upper trapezius, pectoralis major, subscapularis; STRENGTHEN: rear deltoid, rhomboids, mid/low trapezius, infraspinatus, teres minor

### Hips

**Issue: Pelvic tilt in frontal plane (Trendelenburg gait)**
- **Potential Causes:** Weak hip abductors (gluteus medius); spinal scoliosis; leg-length discrepancy; uneven foot arch support

**Issue: Rotated (twisted) pelvis**
- **Potential Causes:** Uneven calf tightness; leg-length discrepancy; uneven foot support; sacroiliac joint injury; muscle imbalance of erector spinae and quadratus lumborum

If your client presents with a tilted and/or rotated pelvis, it is advised to **refer the individual to a clinical specialist** because of the complexity of the issue.

## Summary

- The three phases of running gait are: Drive, Support, Recovery
- Mild pronation is normal; supination and overpronation are considered abnormal foot/gait patterns
- Ground reaction force relates to the force acted upon the body in relation to the force of the foot strike on the ground
- Pronation is **not predictive** of pain or injury
- Q angle relates to the angle of the femur
- Utilization of free energy reduces the active muscle requirement while still producing the required amount of force
- The foot should strike the ground under the runner with the tibia at or close to **90 degrees** in relation to the ground during the foot strike
- The majority of energy when running goes to **supporting the body**
- The hips should rotate slightly in the transverse plane when running — this is a source of energy termed the *spinal engine*
- The PSSS and ASSS systems utilize the stretch-shortening cycle to store potential energy and use kinetic energy
- Excessive vertical body movement equates to wasted energy
- Generally, the faster one runs, the foot strike occurs farther forward on the foot
- A heel strike places more stress on the skeletal system; a midfoot strike places more stress on the musculature system
- Running with a short stride and fast turnover is often preferable to running with a long stride and a slow turnover
