# Module 8: Illness and Injuries

Injuries and illnesses happen, and as a coach, you need to know how to deal with these situations as they arise and know how the training process may be impacted. Running is a sport with a relatively high injury rate, and as such, understanding methodologies to possibly prevent injuries is of critical importance.

## Objectives

Upon completion of this module, you should have an understanding of the following areas:

- Illness/Injury terminology
- What constitutes an emergency situation
- Aspects of injury prevention
- Running injuries
- Tendonitis or tendinosis?
- Role of running surface on impact forces
- Effect of colds/flu on an individual
- Heat-related illnesses
- Hypothermia
- Altitude sickness

## Injury

### Terminology

**Sprain:** Injury to a ligament (ligaments connect bone to bone). Sprains are categorized as Level 1–3, 3 being the most severe.

**Strain:** Injury to muscle or tendon (tendon connects bone to muscle).

**Muscle Cramp (exercise-based):** Involuntary spasm of a muscle or muscles. Cramps can be caused by many factors, including overuse of a muscle in duration and/or intensity and nerve-related issues. Mild dehydration and low electrolyte levels, which were previously associated with cramping, have largely been determined not to be a valid catalyst of cramps (763–765).

**Fracture:** A break in a bone; varying degrees of severity.

**Tendinosis:** (often incorrectly called "tendonitis") Damage to a tendon. Often caused by overuse.

**Bursitis:** Inflammation of a bursa sac. A bursa sac is a synovial fluid-filled sac that is located between bone, muscle, tendons, etc. The purpose of a bursa sac is to provide cushioning and reduce friction.

**Nerve Impingement:** A nerve that has pressure applied to it. Often referred to as a *pinched nerve* or *compressed nerve*.

**RICE:** Acronym for **R**est–**I**ce–**C**ompression–**E**levation.

**Chafing:** Location on the body where the top layer of skin is irritated or worn away either due to skin-on-skin rubbing (e.g., thighs) or more commonly from skin rubbing against clothing (e.g., bra straps). The most common places for chafing are the nipples (men), inner thighs, areas where the bra rubs (women), and under the arms.

To reduce the chance of chafing:

- Try new clothes on short runs before longer runs
- Men: wear nipple covers
- Women: make sure the bra fits properly and is tight
- Apply a skin lubricant such as *Body Glide®* in areas that are likely to get chafed
- Clothing with hidden seams are preferable to garments with regular seams

### Injury Scenarios

**Discomfort that appears to be due to a biomechanical issue:** Work to correct the biomechanical issue to see if the discomfort diminishes or disappears. If not, refer to a specialist.

**Acute pain or trauma that is severe:** Refer to a specialist or emergency care personnel.

**Pain that appears to be the result of overuse:** Reduce duration and intensity. If pain does not diminish or disappear, refer to a specialist.

**Random pain:** Refer to a specialist.

**Discomfort or pain due to illness:** Cease physical activity, and depending on the severity of the illness, the client should contact a doctor.

**Chronic Pain:** Chronic pain is characterized as pain that has been present for six months or more. It is advised to have the client see a medical professional.

The only advice that you can give a client in regard to treatment is **R**est–**I**ce–**C**ompression–**E**levation (RICE). Additionally, you cannot recommend or prescribe medications. This includes over-the-counter medications such as ibuprofen and other anti-inflammatories.

### Emergency Situations

If any of the following situations arise, emergency personnel must be alerted immediately.

> **Note:** Do not leave the individual and/or scene until emergency personnel arrive.

- Broken bone
- Loss of consciousness
- Shock
- Choking
- Cardiac arrest
- Drowning
- Respiratory event (e.g., asthma attack)
- Allergic reaction
- Unresponsiveness
- Uncontrolled bleeding

**Asthma: Inhaler Use** — Contents of an inhaler are a prescription medicine. Therefore, you are not legally allowed to administer the medicine for clients – even if they are suffering an asthma attack. Clients **MUST** administer the drug/inhaler themselves.

## Injury Prevalence and Location

A study by Van Mechelen found that running injuries among recreation runners ranged from 37–56 percent annually (192). Key findings:

- Most running injuries involve the lower extremities
- 50–75 percent of injuries are due to overuse (repetitive stress)
- Recurrences of running injuries range from 20–70 percent

Unclear risk factors (contradictory research):
- Warm-up/Stretching
- Muscular imbalances
- Lack of range of motion
- Orthotics

A 2012 study by Daoud et al. found that 74 percent of collegiate runners experienced a moderate to severe injury annually (648). The most common injuries were:

- Shin splints
  - IT band syndrome
  - Patellofemoral syndrome
  - Achilles tendon pain

### Influence of Foot-Strike Type

Runners who ran with a rear foot strike were **two times more likely** to sustain a repetitive stress injury than those who ran with a midfoot strike (648).

### Anatomical Factors

Two anatomical factors correlate highly with running injuries (649):

1. High-arched feet
2. Leg-length inequality (functional or structural)

### Ultrarunners

A study of 1,212 ultrarunners (650):

- Three-quarters reported having exercise-related injuries the prior year; 65 percent lost at least one training day
- Injured ultrarunners tended to be younger and less experienced
- 3.7 percent reported a stress fracture (48 percent of stress fractures in the feet)

> **'Hard on the Knees'** — A 2013 study by Hinterwimmer et al. found that cartilage changes in beginner marathoners were not clinically significant. Dr. Fredericson notes: *"At faster speeds, runners tend to have better hip mechanics, which leads to reduced loads on the knee."* Running at slower speeds increases cumulative load on the knees. Running with a foot strike closer to the front of the foot reduces cumulative load (773).

## Injury Prevention

### Volume Increases

The traditional **10 percent rule** recommends a maximum 10 percent increase in weekly training volume week-to-week. UESCA's view: 10 percent is a general guideline; changes must be based on individual client feedback. A study by Fields et al. found that only reducing weekly running mileage correlated strongly with decreased running injuries (649).

### Intensity

Intensity and the number of intensity-based workouts should increase incrementally. When intensity is initially added to a program (e.g., hill repeats, fartleks), only one day should be allocated to this purpose. As the body adapts, additional intensity days can be added.

### Cross-training

Cross-training is participating in a sport or activity other than the one competed in for performance enhancement. Common activities:

- Resistance training
- Cycling
- Swimming/water jogging
- Yoga and Pilates
- Other cardiovascular (rowing, elliptical, boxing, soccer, basketball, cross-country skiing)

A 2013 study by Malisoux et al. found that cross-training is a viable strategy to reduce the chance of running injury by varying the load on the musculoskeletal system (687). As most running movements occur in the sagittal and transverse planes, **cross-training in the frontal plane is recommended.**

### Fatigue

When fatigue causes form to break down significantly or the individual cannot maintain focus and clarity, activity level needs to be reduced or ceased. If fatigue level is so high that safety is at risk, exercising must cease.

### Kinesio Tape

*Kinesiology tape* is a stretchable adhesive tape used for injury prevention/intervention or performance enhancement (developed by Dr. Kenzo Kase in 1973). Studies have resulted in largely inconsistent results (207, 209). If clients ask about kinesiology taping, refer them to a specialist.

### Ice

A 2015 study by Leeder et al. found that an ice bath did not promote recovery in subjects with DOMS (678). In fact, icing may slow the healing process. In regard to injury, immediate icing is likely beneficial — but the advice of a medical professional should always be sought.

### Running Shoes

There is no evidence-based research demonstrating that a specific running shoe type reduces injury, but research suggests that **swapping out different types of running shoes during training reduces the risk for injury** (687) by varying the load applied to the musculoskeletal system.

## Running Injuries

Running is synonymous with injury because of one primary factor — impact. Between 37 and 56 percent of runners sustain at least one injury annually (191).

**Four main risk factors** (Marti et al. 1987; Taunton et al. 2002):
1. Running high mileage (25–37 miles/week)
2. Competitive runners with a dedicated focus to training and racing
3. Younger than 34 (runners over 34 were at greater risk for Achilles and calf injuries)
4. Newly active (active less than 8.5 years)

**Other factors (205):**
- **Weight:** Bell-shaped curve — substantially under or overweight increased risk
- **Shoes:** Shoe type did not affect injury rate
- **Gender:** Women are at higher risk than men
  - Women: knee, hip, shin
  - Men: patellar and Achilles tendons

### What Is Tendonitis?

The term *tendonitis* means inflammation of a tendon. However, recent research has shown that in conditions diagnosed as tendonitis, there is **little to no inflammatory cells present** (606). The term **tendinosis** is more applicable. The primary finding is a disruption of collagen fibers of the tendon, not inflammation.

Common treatment methods such as anti-inflammatory medications and corticosteroid injections have been found to **increase recovery time** and accelerate the degenerative process (603–606). Eccentric strengthening has been shown to assist in increasing the collagen and strength of a tendon (603, 604).

### Common Causes of Running Injuries

1. **Poor Running Mechanics** — foot-strike type, excessive pronation/supination; retraining is a long process
2. **Worn Shoes** — primarily an issue for heel strikers; excessive wear is likely a contributing factor for injury
3. **Lack of Range of Motion** — tight hamstrings are often the primary cause; especially true with heel strikers
4. **Mileage and Intensity** — starting with too much mileage or increasing too fast; *Metabolic fitness precedes structural readiness* (190)
5. **Muscle Imbalances** — substantial imbalances are not only inefficient but potentially injurious

**Key program guidelines:**
- Always warm up properly
- Be aware of high-volume increases
- Speed work: start with only one day/week, maximum two days/week
- Work to have your client run with a proper running gait
- Do not start speed work until a solid base mileage has been established
- If your client experiences muscular/skeletal discomfort, have the person stop and RICE; if pain continues, refer to a specialist
- Allow for enough rest
- Cross-train
- Train in frontal and transverse planes, not just the sagittal plane
- Listen to the body

### Common Running Injuries

- **Iliotibial Band Syndrome (ITB syndrome)** — pain on the lateral aspect of the knee caused by the IT band rubbing against the lateral femoral epicondyle
- **Runner's Knee (patellofemoral syndrome, PFS)** — pain under the kneecap caused by tight connective tissue and patellar malalignment; tight quadriceps, IT bands, and lack of gluteal strength contribute
- **Shin Splints** — pain in the region of the tibia; three types:
  - **Muscular (exertional compartment syndrome):** tibialis anterior affected; pain subsides when running stops
  - **Skeletal (medial tibial stress syndrome, MTSS):** injury or stress to the tibia bone; continuing to run can lead to a stress fracture
  - **Tibial Fascia (Tibial Fasciitis):** inflammation of deep fascia surrounding the tibia (442)
- **Hamstring Injuries** — caused by hyperextension of the knee; higher risk with heel strikers due to longer stride; anterior pelvic tilt increases risk
- **Ankle injuries** — twisted/rolled/sprained ankle; Achilles tendinosis refers to damage of the Achilles tendon
- **Popliteus Injury** — small muscle behind the knee that "unlocks" the knee, prevents hyperextension, and laterally rotates the femur; injury causes knee extension pain
- **Plantar Fasciitis** — pain at beginning of foot arch closest to heel; tight calf muscles often contribute; weak intrinsic foot muscles may increase load (600)

### What Is a Side Stitch?

Also known as *exercise-related transient abdominal pain* (ETAP) (437). Characterized by cramping or sharp pain toward the bottom of the rib cage, most commonly on the right side. No single cause identified. Common treatments: stopping exercise, stretching, deep breathing.

### Other Running-Related Injuries

- **Bunion (hallux valgus)** — abnormal bone growth causing the big toe to slant inward; severe cases require surgery; advised to run in shoes with ample room in the toe box (651)
- **Morton's Neuroma** — thickening of tissue around a nerve in the ball of the foot; burning sensation; associated with high-heeled shoes; lower heel and wider toe box may help (652)
- **Sesamoiditis** — irritation of the tendon in which sesamoid bones are embedded; rest and a dancer's pad can assist recovery (653)

### Running Recovery

During recovery, running should not be done on back-to-back days. If a client has a strain or sprain, there must be complete recovery before resuming running.

NSAIDs such as ibuprofen and aspirin have been found to slow muscle recovery and muscle growth (134). A 2015 study found that taking ibuprofen did not help prolong run time (676). A 2013 study by Kuster et al. found that ingesting over-the-counter pain relievers prior to a marathon resulted in increased gastrointestinal issues and muscle cramps (677). **NSAIDs are not recommended for use prior to competition unless directed by a physician.**

### Running Surface

Surface hierarchy from best to worst (lowest to highest impact):

| Rank | Surface |
|---|---|
| **Best** | Grass |
| | Dirt |
| | Rubber Track |
| | Asphalt |
| **Worst** | Cement |

Studies found grass produced the least foot pressure; other surfaces were comparable (278–280). Treadmill running resulted in the **lowest magnitude of plantar pressure** of all surfaces tested (281).

### Blisters

Two common causes:
1. Friction between skin and sock/shoe
2. Wet feet due to water or excessive sweating

Ways to prevent blisters (292):
- Make sure feet are dry
- Wear socks that wick sweat
- Do not shave off calluses
- Run only in properly fitted shoes
- Do not lace up shoes too tight
- Use a small amount of skin lubricant (Vaseline® or Body Glide®)

### Summary

The two factors that most all studies agree on as significant contributing factors for running injury are **high mileage** and **increasing mileage too fast.**

## Illness

### Terminology

**Hyponatremia:** Electrolyte imbalance where sodium levels are lower than normal. Can result from overhydration.

**Dehydration:** Occurs when the body takes in less fluid than it uses.

**Hypothermia:** Occurs when the body's core temperature drops below what is necessary for body function and metabolism to take place at a normal rate.

**Heat Illnesses:** A range of heat-related illnesses from fairly benign (cramps) to heat stroke, which can be fatal.

---

## Colds/Flu

Endurance exercise has been shown to decrease one's immune system. When sick, the body undergoes physiological responses that decrease its ability to perform (296):

1. **The body breaks down muscle (catabolism)** — microscopic damage to muscles with fever; muscle strength decreases and can take up to two weeks to regain
2. **Fat metabolism is decreased** — body primarily utilizes muscle protein for fuel
3. **Aerobic metabolism is diminished** — VO₂ max and lactate threshold decrease

When fighting off a cold or flu, exercise should not be done. Focus must be on rest and recovery.

### Guidelines

- If clients are symptomatic (runny nose, elevated temperature, congestion, body ache, chills), they should **not exercise**
- If head cold has "moved" into the chest, it is **imperative not to exercise**
- Rest and recovery until the client feels better and shows no signs of illness

## Heat-Related Illnesses

Three categories:

1. **Heat Cramps**
2. **Heat Exhaustion**
3. **Heat Stroke**

The body has approximately 2–4 million sweat glands. Exercising in hot and humid conditions can prove dangerous because of a lack of evaporative cooling.

> **Wiping away sweat before it has had a chance to evaporate greatly reduces the cooling benefit of sweating.**

### Heat Cramps

Characterized by muscle cramps and sweating. Treatment: cessation of activity, moving to a cool shaded area, drinking an electrolyte-based beverage, stretching and massaging the cramped area.

### Heat Exhaustion

Occurs when the body's natural temperature regulation system begins to break down. Common symptoms (297):

- Dizziness
- Heavy sweating
- Confusion
- Nausea
- Weak, rapid pulse
- Low blood pressure
- Excessive thirst
- Hyperventilation
- Loss of appetite
- Anxiety

Treatment: stop activity, move to shady/cool location, give electrolyte drink, cool with fan/wet cloths, supine position with legs elevated, call medical personnel.

### Heat Stroke

Most serious; can be fatal. Symptoms (297):

- Rapid heart rate
- No sweating
- High body temperature (>103°F)
- Red, dry skin
- Confusion
- Vomiting
- Nausea
- Erratic behavior
- Difficulty breathing
- Constricted pupils

**Lower body temperature immediately and call 911.**

### Prevention

Stay properly hydrated, acclimate to heat gradually, take breaks, wear proper clothing (light colors, wicking), prehydrate with cold fluids prior to training/racing in heat (592).

### Heat Acclimatization

Key factors:

1. Individuals differ greatly in ability to deal with heat and humidity
2. More body fat = less efficient at dissipating heat
3. Larger runners require more energy to reduce body heat
4. Wind speed, humidity, and shade all affect the runner (311)
5. Acclimatization must be done slowly — start with 10–15 minutes/day of light exercise and progressively increase (595)
6. Heat acclimation takes approximately **two to four weeks** (90); after 10 days, sweating capacity nearly doubles (596)
7. Novice athletes and deconditioned individuals are often most negatively affected by hot weather
8. Physiological benefits of heat acclimatization are lost **2–3 weeks** after returning to a temperate environment (594)

Heat-acclimatized individuals can sweat up to **4 liters/hour**, while non-acclimatized individuals sweat approximately **1.5 liters/hour** (732).

## Hypothermia

Hypothermia = body temperature of 95°F or less (normal = 98.6°F). Water increases heat loss — wear wicking clothing when running in cold weather.

Three stages (451):

### Mild Hypothermia
Most common symptom: shivering and cold/numb extremities. Treatment: move to warm area, remove wet clothes, put on dry clothing.

### Moderate Hypothermia
Symptoms: excessive shivering, loss of muscular coordination, lips and ears turning blue. Must be immediately moved to warm area and changed into dry clothing. Medical attention required.

### Severe Hypothermia
Body begins to shut down — heart rate, blood pressure, and respiration rate all decline substantially. Unless moved to warm area with medical attention, organs shut down and death will result. **Call for emergency medical attention.**

### Prevention

- Be aware of weather forecast
- Wear sweat-wicking fabrics, dress in layers
- Cover extremities and exposed body parts in extreme cold
- Always train with a partner
- Carry a cell phone
- Train in a concentrated area near home

## High Altitude

If a race is at high altitude (6,000 ft or above) and your client lives at or near sea level, arrive a **minimum of three or four days** ahead of the race to acclimate. Common symptoms of altitude sickness:

- Headaches
- Dizzy and light-headed
- Nausea
- Excessive shortness of breath
- Weakness and fatigue
- Bloody nose (often due to dry air)

Individuals tend to become dehydrated faster at high altitudes. Consuming slightly higher amounts of water might help (298). The only reliable way to reduce altitude sickness is to **go to a lower altitude.**

> *Hydration-related illnesses/conditions such as dehydration and hyponatremia are discussed in greater detail in the Sports Nutrition module.*

## Summary

### Injury

- In emergency situations, emergency personnel must be called (911)
- As a certified UESCA coach, you must be CPR/AED certified
- To mitigate injury chance: monitor volume increases, intensity-level increases, fatigue level, and integrate cross-training
- Running has a high injury rate primarily because of the impact nature of the activity
- Treadmills have the lowest impact on the feet, followed by grass surfaces
- There is no definitive cause of side stitches
- If your client suffers a sports-related injury, refer to a physical therapist or sports medicine physician
- You cannot diagnose a client's injury; the only treatment advice you can give is RICE

### Illness

- If your client has a cold and is symptomatic, the person should not exercise
- When sick, the body becomes catabolic and fat metabolism decreases
- The three types of heat illnesses are: heat cramps, heat exhaustion, and heat stroke (heat stroke is most serious and can be fatal)
- Heat acclimatization must be done gradually and safely; advised by a physician
- The three levels of hypothermia are mild, moderate, and severe (severe hypothermia can be fatal)
- To avoid altitude sickness, a progressive acclimatization program is strongly suggested
- Individuals acclimate at different rates
